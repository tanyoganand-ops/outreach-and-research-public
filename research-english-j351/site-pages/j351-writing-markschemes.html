1. How the assessment works

For J351/01 Section B, AO5 is worth 24 marks and AO6 is worth 16. AO5 assesses communication, form, purpose, audience and organisation. AO6 assesses vocabulary, sentence structures, spelling and punctuation. Each is judged separately using best fit.

AO5 has six levels, spanning 1-4, 5-8, 9-12, 13-16, 17-20 and 21-24. AO6 has four, spanning 1-4, 5-8, 9-12 and 13-16. Zero is available for no response worthy of credit. Read OCR's actual grid for the descriptors; this page does not substitute a simplified home-made grid for it.

A missing headline, an isolated punctuation slip or a memorable final sentence does not have a fixed automatic mark consequence. Assessment considers the evidence across the response. A qualification grade cannot be read straight off a single writing-task score.

2. One original practice task, three short extracts

Original practice brief: write an article for your school magazine arguing for a change to the school day. These are invented teaching extracts. Some factual-sounding claims are deliberately left open to challenge. They should not be treated as verified research.

For each extract, identify one AO5 choice, one AO6 feature and one useful revision. Do not award a score out of 40 from these extracts alone.

Extract A: original teaching text

“I think school should start later because everyone is tired in the morning, its a fact that teenagers need more sleep than adults and my first lesson is basically a write-off anyway. Lots of schools in other countrys start later and there results are better. Also break times should be longer because 20 minutes is not enough time to eat and relax and get to your next lesson on time, teachers always say being late is disrespectful but the timetable makes it impossible. In conclusion school should start later and breaks should be longer, this would be better for everyone.”

Review notes: the position is clear, but reasons are mostly asserted rather than explained. The school-magazine audience could be more deliberately addressed. Check the comma splices and the spellings/word choices "its", "countrys" and "there results". The claims about other countries and improved results are unsupported in this invented extract; a revision should substantiate, qualify or replace them.

Extract B: original teaching text

“Every morning, registration takes the register of people who are not really there. We sit upright, we answer to our names, and we learn almost nothing - because the science on teenage sleep is no longer controversial, only inconvenient. I am not asking for a lie-in. I am asking the timetable to meet us where our bodies are. A 9.30 start would cost one lesson’s length from the day; in return, the school would gain five lessons’ worth of awake students. The change is small. The yawning is enormous. The choice is obvious.”

Review notes: the opening image and direct statement of purpose give the extract a clear voice. Ask whether the timetable arithmetic and claims about science are supported and whether the practical consequences are developed. "The choice is obvious." is a complete sentence: "The choice" is its subject and "is obvious" its predicate. It is not a fragment. This short extract cannot demonstrate a whole answer's sustained development or justify a numerical mark.

Extract C: original teaching text

“At 8.58 every morning, six hundred of us perform a small piece of theatre called Being Awake. The lights are on, the registers are called, and behind our eyes the audience is empty. I know, because last term I fell asleep in a lesson I love, taught by a teacher I rate, in a chair designed by someone who had clearly never met a spine. Here is the uncomfortable part: nobody in this building is lazy. We are just scheduled like adults and built like teenagers, and we spend the first two periods of every day paying the difference. Move the start to 9.30 and the theatre ends; the learning - the real kind, the kind with the lights on behind the eyes - can finally begin.”

Review notes: the theatre image connects the opening to the closing proposal; the first-person anecdote and shifts in sentence length are deliberate choices. The final sentence does contain a semicolon joining related clauses. Consider what practical reasoning or alternative view a full article might still need. The anecdote is fictional practice material, not real candidate testimony. No examiner mark has been awarded.

3. Upgrade the writing, not the device count

  1. Form and audience: choose a suitable headline if useful, and make the article's relationship with school-magazine readers clear.
  2. Development: choose a relevant reason and explain it with credible support or an explicitly hypothetical example. Avoid unsourced claims about research or exam results.
  3. Reasoning: consider a practical consequence or opposing view if it helps the purpose. A concession is optional, not an upper-level requirement.
  4. Accuracy: separate accidental run-ons, correct agreement and spellings, then choose sentence structures that make the point clear.
One possible original revision, not a graded answer:

A timetable worth discussing

If the first lesson of the day feels difficult, a later start may sound like an easy answer. But any proposal needs to explain what would happen at the other end of the day. A short consultation could ask students and staff about travel, clubs and family responsibilities before a limited trial is considered. We should discuss whether a different timetable would help, rather than assume that changing the clock solves every problem.

4. Review a full response

Check whether ideas develop, paragraphs connect and the tone fits the reader throughout. Check technical control from the first sentence to the last. You do not need exactly one main argument, exactly two sentence-length changes, a compulsory concession or a prescribed type of final line.

Use the AO5/AO6 checklist to choose your next revision. If an assessment is needed, use the whole response and OCR's official descriptors, and keep any practice judgement separate from an examiner-awarded mark.
