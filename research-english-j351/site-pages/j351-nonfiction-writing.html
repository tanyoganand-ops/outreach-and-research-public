Communicating information and ideas: choose the right voice, develop relevant ideas and make the whole piece work for its reader.

The current task: J351/01 Section B offers two non-fiction writing tasks. Answer one. Each is worth 40 marks: AO5 contributes 24 and AO6 contributes 16. OCR's Assessment Story identifies letter, article and speech/talk for tasks from 2019 onwards.

1. What the assessment rewards

AO5: clear, effective communication; choices of tone, style and register that suit the form, purpose and audience; information and ideas organised into a coherent whole. Content and organisation both matter.

AO6: vocabulary and sentence structures chosen for clarity and effect, with accurate spelling and punctuation. Accuracy is assessed across the response, not by counting devices.

OCR uses best-fit level judgements, with AO5 and AO6 assessed separately. One striking opening is not enough to establish a mark for an entire response.

2. Read the actual brief

  1. Identify the form, reader/listener and main purpose. Some tasks combine purposes, for example explaining a problem before advising someone.
  2. Decide what the reader needs to understand, feel or do by the end. Pick ideas that serve that aim.
  3. Use the task's scaffolding bullets to support your planning. OCR describes them as guidance and support, not a universal fixed-number paragraph template. Remain responsive to the whole task.
  4. Put related ideas together and choose an order that builds understanding or conviction. The plan may change with the task.

3. Make the form recognisable

Letter

Use an appropriate greeting, write directly to the recipient and end with a suitable sign-off. A named recipient commonly takes "Yours sincerely"; an unnamed formal recipient commonly takes "Yours faithfully". Match the relationship and purpose.

Do not spend exam time inventing addresses. OCR's June 2025 report says an appropriate salutation and valediction are enough. Addresses are unnecessary; never include your personal address.

Article

A relevant headline can help signal the form. Establish a clear angle and a voice that suits the publication's readers. Develop the piece through purposeful paragraphs. Subheadings can help some articles, but are not a universal requirement.

Speech or talk

Write words that can be spoken to the stated audience. An appropriate greeting or direct address, clear transitions and an ending that serves the purpose can help. Do not rely on stage directions or a compulsory applause line.

Form matters, but avoid invented penalties. OCR's June 2024 report describes responses in an inappropriate form receiving a mark towards the lower end of a level while still achieving for purpose. It does not establish an automatic whole-level deduction for every form error.

4. Register: the same subject, different readers

The two existing original openings below illustrate differences in address, vocabulary and sentence rhythm. They are constructed practice writing about data privacy, not checked factual accounts of a particular app or advertising system.

For a teenage magazine (original): “Your phone knows you better than your best mate does. It knows you rewatch that video at 1am, that you zoom into photos, that you linger on things you would never admit to liking. And every night it sells that version of you to the highest bidder. Still feel like doom-scrolling?”
For a broadsheet comment piece (original): “The device in your pocket maintains a more faithful biography of you than any archivist could: your hesitations, your returns, the searches you typed and deleted. We have accepted, without debate, an arrangement in which the most intimate record of our inner lives is a commercial asset. It is worth asking who signed that contract, and on our behalf.”

Compare what each voice assumes about the reader. Which words would you change for a headteacher, local newspaper or younger audience?

5. Techniques are choices, not a quota

  • A rhetorical question can open a piece, create a transition or challenge an assumption, if it suits the purpose. It is not restricted to one position.
  • Repetition, a three-part phrase, an anecdote or a concession can help, but none is compulsory in every answer.
  • Examples should support the point. Do not present invented statistics, research or authorities as verified evidence. A clearly hypothetical situation can illustrate an idea.
  • Use a semicolon only when the sentence calls for one. A deliberate fragment is not an automatic route to marks; uncontrolled fragments weaken clarity.
  • An ending might propose action, bring ideas together, leave a question or return to an earlier image. It need not sound like a slogan.

6. Existing original practice tasks and openings

These are brief teaching examples, not full exam answers and not OCR questions. Names, locations, events and numbers are fictional details for the exercise. The labels below do not imply an awarded mark.

Task A (original): Write an article for your local newspaper arguing that a public space in your town should be protected from redevelopment.
Model opening (original): “Every town has one place that holds its memory. For us it is the lido on Mill Lane: forty metres of cold green water where three generations learned to swim, where the weeds are winning and the council sees a car park. On Tuesday the consultation closes, and unless the town speaks, the diggers will do the talking. They always do.”
Task B (original): Write a letter to your headteacher persuading them to ban single-use plastic bottles in school.
Model opening (original): “Dear Dr Araya, I am writing because last week I watched our canteen sell four hundred bottles of water in a single lunch break, and then watched four hundred bottles head for three already-full bins. Our school teaches us the science of what plastic does. I think it is time the school acted on its own lessons, and I am writing to ask you to make that happen.”

What to notice: Task A establishes local stakes and a clear viewpoint. Task B addresses a decision-maker and makes a request. A full response would still need developed ideas, suitable organisation and sustained technical control. Do not copy their fictional numbers into a claim about a real school or place.

7. Check the whole response

Leave time within your own exam plan to reread. Check sentence boundaries, agreement, punctuation, spelling and missing words. There is no sentence-length threshold that makes a sentence wrong. Read long sentences carefully, but check short ones too.

Ask whether each paragraph adds something and whether the tone still fits the reader at the end. See the dedicated checklist and flexible structures guide linked above.
