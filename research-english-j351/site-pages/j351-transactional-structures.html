Choose a structure that helps this audience with this purpose. Do not force every task into the same paragraph pattern.

Newly written teaching guide and worked examples, 22 November 2026. These are not recovered historical files, official candidate scripts or marked exemplars.

1. Start from the outcome

Ask: what should the reader understand or do by the end? Then choose the material and sequence that make that outcome possible. A task can ask you to explain, inform, advise, argue or persuade in combination. The form tells you how to present the response; the purpose tells you what it needs to achieve.

2. Flexible routes for different purposes

Inform or explain

Establish the subject and why it matters; organise the useful information into related groups; explain connections, causes or consequences; end by bringing the main understanding together. Define unfamiliar terms where the reader needs them. A collection of facts without explanation may not fulfil an explanatory purpose.

Advise

Recognise the reader's situation; offer manageable actions; explain how and why those actions might help; consider a likely obstacle; end with a realistic next step. Advice should be more useful than a string of commands.

Argue

Set out a clear position; develop reasons and relevant support; examine an alternative if doing so helps the argument; reach a considered conclusion. More than one developed reason is allowed. A concession is useful when relevant, not a condition of reaching a high level.

Persuade

Connect with the reader's interests; make the desired action or change clear; develop the benefits and address practical doubts; end with a suitable request or invitation. Emotional language can help, but pressure alone is not developed reasoning.

These are teaching routes, not OCR-prescribed paragraph counts. Combine or change them when the actual task calls for it. A piece can shift purpose as it develops while remaining coherent.

3. Adapt the route to the form

  • Letter: speak to the named role or recipient, explain why this person should care, and make any request proportionate to their power to act. Use a greeting and sign-off, not invented addresses.
  • Article: establish an angle that fits the publication. Guide readers through ideas with purposeful paragraphs. A headline can help; a fixed headline formula cannot replace development.
  • Speech/talk: make the movement of ideas easy to hear. A greeting, audience contact, clear transitions and a purposeful final appeal can help, without requiring a fixed number of questions or repeated phrases.

One possible speech plan, not a template to memorise

Original practice brief: persuade your year group to help look after a shared school garden. Open with a concrete picture of a space everyone uses; explain one achievable improvement; show how different volunteers could contribute; acknowledge time or experience concerns; finish with a clear invitation to a planning meeting. Change the plan if your audience or purpose changes.

Original worked example 1: persuasive letter

Original practice brief: Write a letter to your headteacher persuading them to trial a short after-school library opening. This is an invented teaching task, not an OCR past question.

Dear Ms Patel,

When the final bell rings, the school library closes at almost the same moment that some students finally have time to use it. I am writing to ask whether we could trial opening it for half an hour after school on two afternoons each week. A quiet place to work should not disappear just when it becomes most useful.

For a student who shares a bedroom, or whose journey home involves waiting for a later bus, that short opening could make a practical difference. Imagine trying to finish a difficult paragraph with a younger sibling beside you and a television in the next room. The problem is not a lack of effort. Sometimes it is a lack of somewhere to put that effort.

The library could also help students ask for the right support. Someone who has forgotten a book could find an alternative; someone who has reached a dead end could at least leave with a clearer question for the next lesson. The aim would be focused, independent work, not another compulsory club.

I recognise that staffing and supervision cannot simply be wished into existence. That is why I am proposing a limited trial rather than an immediate permanent change. Students could sign up in advance, follow clear rules and help gather feedback. The school could then judge whether enough students use the space to justify continuing.

Would you consider discussing the proposal with the librarian and student council? If the trial proved impractical, we would understand why. If it worked, half an hour could turn an otherwise wasted wait into a useful part of the school day.

Yours sincerely,

A student

How this example is built

  • Opening: names a specific practical problem and a limited request. The headteacher can see what decision is being asked for.
  • Development: the hypothetical shared-room situation explains who might benefit and why. It does not claim to report a real survey.
  • Audience: the staffing paragraph responds to a decision-maker's likely concerns. A trial gives the proposal a practical shape.
  • Ending: asks for a discussion and explains how the proposal could be evaluated, rather than promising guaranteed results.
  • AO6: a short sentence sharpens the distinction between effort and space; a semicolon connects two related examples. These are purposeful choices, not required items.

Limit: this has not been examiner-marked. Its value is in the choices you can inspect, not a claimed level or score. No addresses are needed.

Original worked example 2: advisory article

Original practice brief: Write an article for a teenage magazine advising readers how to make more deliberate use of their phones. This is an invented teaching task, not an OCR past question.

Your phone does not need every spare minute

You sit down to answer one message. A few videos later, the short break has swallowed the time you meant to spend doing something else. If that sounds familiar, the answer need not be a dramatic promise to abandon your phone. It might be a smaller decision about when it deserves your attention.

Start by choosing one moment you want back. Perhaps it is the walk home with a friend, the first part of your homework or the last few minutes before you go to sleep. Trying to change every habit at once can make the task feel impossible. One clear boundary gives you something you can actually notice.

Then make that boundary easier to keep. If you want to work without checking messages, put the phone somewhere you cannot reach without getting up. If a group chat makes you feel that every message needs an immediate answer, tell your friends when you will check it. You are not rejecting them. You are making room to give one thing your full attention.

Of course, phones are useful. They hold travel plans, music, jokes and conversations we value. That is why a total ban may miss the point. The useful question is not whether screens are good or bad, but whether the way you are using yours matches what you meant to do.

Expect an imperfect start. If you catch yourself scrolling again, stop and reset rather than deciding that the whole plan has failed. At the end of the week, ask what changed. Did you finish work with fewer interruptions? Did a conversation last longer? Keep the boundary that helped and adjust the one that did not.

Your phone can stay part of your life without taking charge of every pause in it. Choose one moment. Keep it for yourself.

How this example is built

  • Opening: uses a recognisable situation and establishes a manageable approach rather than an absolute rule.
  • Sequence: choose a moment, change the surroundings, consider the complication, then review the result. The order is useful to someone trying the advice.
  • Audience: direct address and familiar situations suit a teenage magazine without assuming every reader has the same habits.
  • Balance: acknowledging useful phone functions makes the advice less blunt. It is relevant here, not a compulsory concession for every article.
  • Ending: returns to the idea of choosing a moment. The short closing sentences are complete sentences, not fragments.

Limit: practical teaching advice, not a medical claim or an examiner-graded answer. Another effective response could use a different order, voice or set of examples.

4. Practise changing the choices

  1. Turn the library letter into a speech to students. Keep the main proposal, but change what the audience can do and how you speak to them.
  2. Turn the phone article into a letter to a younger sibling. Keep useful advice, but adapt the relationship and level of detail.
  3. Write an alternative ending that still fulfils the purpose without a slogan, a rhetorical question or a call-back.
  4. Highlight one paragraph where explanation develops the idea, then improve a paragraph that merely states an opinion.
  5. Read your response aloud to find unclear sentence boundaries. Do not add a semicolon or fragment simply to tick a box.

5. Review without guessing a grade

Use the AO5/AO6 checklist. Identify evidence of effective choices and a specific change that would help the reader. A full assessment needs the whole response and the relevant official grid; these short teaching annotations do not award marks, levels or qualification grades.
