A practical bank of phrases, techniques, theme vocabulary and flexible routes for OCR J351/01 Section B. Choose what fits the actual task. Do not try to use everything.

For revision and teacher-approved practice. This is not an authorised aid for a formal exam. Every phrase, task and worked example here is original teaching material, not an official model answer or a guarantee of marks.

Start here: a quick planning route

  1. Read: choose one task. Identify the form, audience and the complete purpose, including any linked requests.
  2. Decide: what should this reader understand, feel or do at the end?
  3. Select: choose relevant ideas you can explain. Use credible support or clearly hypothetical examples, not invented research.
  4. Order: choose a route from the purpose sections, then adapt it to the form and theme.
  5. Write and check: develop ideas, connect paragraphs, sustain the voice and check sentence control.
Assessment essentials: answer one of two non-fiction writing tasks. Letter, article and speech/talk are the forms listed in OCR's current Assessment Story from 2019 onwards. AO5 is 24 marks and AO6 is 16. There is no compulsory rhetorical-device quota or universal paragraph formula.

1. Phrase banks by purpose

Purposes can overlap. A persuasive letter may explain a problem before making a request; an informative article may include advice. Follow the complete task rather than forcing it into a single label.

Argue: reach a reasoned judgement

Possible route: Position → developed reasons → a useful alternative view → judgement. Change the order if the brief needs it.

  • State a view: "The strongest reason to support [proposal] is [reason]."
  • Narrow the issue: "The question is not simply whether [A], but whether [B]."
  • Develop: "This matters because [consequence for this reader]."
  • Illustrate honestly: "Consider a student who [clearly hypothetical situation]."
  • Qualify: "This may help when [condition], although it will not solve [limit]."
  • Consider another view: "It is reasonable to worry about [objection]. However, [reasoned response]."
  • Weigh: "The benefit of [X] needs to be weighed against [cost]."
  • Conclude: "On balance, [judgement], provided that [condition]."

Persuade: move this reader towards action

Possible route: Connect to a shared concern → show what should change → make it seem worthwhile and practical → ask for action.

  • Connect: "We all want [shared aim]. The difficulty is [problem]."
  • Make the request: "I am asking you to consider [specific action]."
  • Show the benefit: "For [group], this would mean [concrete improvement]."
  • Make stakes concrete: "Without [change], [credible consequence] is likely to continue."
  • Offer a route: "We could begin with [manageable first step]."
  • Meet a concern: "Rather than commit immediately to [large change], we could trial [smaller change]."
  • Invite: "If you can offer [time/skill], you can help by [action]."
  • Close: "Please [specific request], so that [purpose]."

Advise: help the reader make useful choices

Possible route: Recognise the situation → manageable actions with reasons → likely obstacle → realistic next step.

  • Acknowledge: "If [situation] feels difficult, start with [small action]."
  • Prioritise: "Before you decide [X], think about [important factor]."
  • Explain advice: "Try [action], because it can help you [benefit]."
  • Offer choice: "If [option A] is not possible, [option B] may work better."
  • Prevent a problem: "Avoid [action] when [condition]; instead, [alternative]."
  • Reassure without promising: "You do not have to [extreme demand] to begin making progress."
  • Review: "After [reasonable period], ask yourself whether [useful measure]."
  • Close: "Choose the step that fits your situation and build from there."

Explain: make a connection clear

Possible route: Establish what happens → show how or why → illustrate the connection → explain its importance.

  • Define: "By [term], I mean [clear explanation]."
  • Identify a cause: "One reason for [effect] is [cause]."
  • Show a mechanism: "When [event] happens, [next step], which can lead to [effect]."
  • Distinguish: "This is different from [related idea], because [difference]."
  • Illustrate: "For example, imagine [clearly hypothetical example]."
  • Complicate: "The result also depends on [factor]."
  • Draw a consequence: "As a result, [effect on the relevant person/group]."
  • Bring together: "Understanding [cause] helps explain why [outcome]."

Inform: select and organise what the reader needs

Possible route: Establish the subject → group useful information → clarify practical details → draw the information together.

  • Orient: "This article sets out [subject] for [reader's need]."
  • Organise: "There are several aspects worth considering, beginning with [aspect]."
  • Clarify: "In practical terms, this means [detail]."
  • Add relevant detail: "Another point to bear in mind is [information]."
  • Compare: "Unlike [A], [B] involves [difference]."
  • Flag a limit: "The arrangements may vary according to [condition]."
  • Guide attention: "For anyone planning to [action], the main consideration is [factor]."
  • Summarise: "The main point to remember is [accurate summary]."

2. Phrase banks by form

These are optional ways to signal the form. Content, audience and purpose still determine whether the piece works.

Letter

Use an appropriate greeting, address the actual recipient, establish the purpose, develop relevant points, and end with a suitable sign-off. No invented addresses.

  • Formal opening: "Dear [name/title],"
  • Purpose: "I am writing to ask whether you would consider [proposal]."
  • Recipient focus: "As [role], you are in a position to [relevant action]."
  • Measured disagreement: "I understand the intention behind [decision], but I am concerned about [effect]."
  • Practical close: "I would welcome the opportunity to discuss [next step]."
  • Sign-off: "Yours sincerely, [for a named recipient]; Yours faithfully, [for an unnamed formal recipient]."

Article

Fit the publication and its readers. A relevant headline can establish the angle; purposeful paragraphs develop it. A headline alone does not make a successful article.

  • Question headline: "Who gets to decide how we use [shared resource]?"
  • Contrast headline: "More [benefit], less [cost]"
  • Scene opening: "At [time/place], [small concrete observation]."
  • Reader connection: "You may have noticed [recognisable situation]."
  • Shift in scale: "What looks like a small inconvenience can become [wider issue]."
  • Close: "Perhaps the better question is [thought that follows from the argument]."

Speech or talk

Write for listeners, with language they can follow as they hear it. Signal changes in direction and make any final request clear.

  • Greeting: "Good morning, [audience]."
  • Shared experience: "Think about the last time you [relevant experience]."
  • Purpose: "I want to explain why [issue] matters to us."
  • Signpost: "Let us begin with [point]. Then consider [next point]."
  • Reframe: "This is not a request for [misunderstanding]. It is a request for [actual proposal]."
  • Final invitation: "If we want [shared aim], we can start by [specific action]. Thank you."

3. Techniques: purpose, example and restraint

Choose a technique because of the effect it creates, not because a checklist says to include it. Plain, accurate language can be more effective than forced decoration.

Direct address

Use: Connect with a particular reader or listener.

Original example: "You should not have to choose between a quiet place to study and a long wait for the bus."

Keep control: Do not assume every reader has the same experience.

Concrete detail

Use: Make an abstract point easier to picture.

Original example: "The last chair is stacked away just as the late-bus students arrive."

Keep control: Use a clearly invented scene for practice, not a false report of a real event.

Rhetorical question

Use: Invite thought, open an angle or challenge an assumption.

Original example: "What use is a shared space if it is closed when people need it?"

Keep control: Too many questions can replace reasoning rather than strengthen it.

Contrast

Use: Make a distinction clear.

Original example: "The problem is not a lack of effort. It is a lack of space."

Keep control: Avoid a false either/or when the issue has more possibilities.

Repetition / parallel phrasing

Use: Give a key point emphasis and an audible pattern.

Original example: "A place to read. A place to think. A place to begin again."

Keep control: This deliberately uses fragments. Use complete sentences elsewhere and do not force the pattern.

Three-part list

Use: Group related ideas memorably.

Original example: "The proposal should be affordable, accessible and realistic."

Keep control: The list must add meaning; three adjectives are not automatic marks.

Anecdote or hypothetical scene

Use: Show how an issue affects someone.

Original example: "Imagine arriving early for a club and finding nowhere quiet to wait."

Keep control: Make a hypothetical example clear. Do not invent research or personal testimony and pass it off as verified.

Concession and response

Use: Consider a reasonable complication.

Original example: "Staff time is limited. That is why a short trial is more realistic than opening every afternoon."

Keep control: Optional: use it when it helps the purpose, not in every paragraph.

Analogy / metaphor

Use: Clarify an idea through a useful comparison.

Original example: "A timetable is a map of the school day; changing one route affects the others."

Keep control: Keep the comparison consistent and do not let it replace practical explanation.

Call-back

Use: Bring an earlier image or idea into the ending.

Original example: "If the opening focuses on a locked door, the ending can return to who could open it."

Keep control: Not every ending needs a call-back. A direct request may work better.

Modal verbs and qualification

Use: Match confidence to the strength of the claim.

Original example: "A trial could reveal whether the change helps."

Keep control: Could, may and might express possibilities; will and must make stronger claims.

Sentence length and punctuation

Use: Control pace and clarify relationships.

Original example: "A trial is manageable; a permanent change needs more evidence."

Keep control: A semicolon joins closely related independent clauses here. Never add one simply to meet a quota.

4. Vocabulary and routes by theme

These themes are practice categories, not a prediction of the exam topic. Learn meaning and collocation together. A precise familiar word is better than an impressive word used wrongly. The routes connect theme, purpose and form; each can be adapted.

Education and opportunity

  • accessible: easy to reach or use. accessible study spaces.
  • equitable: fair while recognising different needs. equitable access to support.
  • attainment: educational achievement. improve attainment.
  • autonomy: the ability to make decisions for yourself. develop student autonomy.
  • constructive: intended to help or improve. constructive feedback.
  • feasible: possible in practice. a feasible timetable change.

Form + purpose route: Persuade a headteacher: identify a barrier → show who it affects → offer a practical trial → ask how it will be assessed.

Phrase in context: Advice article: "Choose a revision routine that is realistic enough to repeat."

Technology and media

  • intrusive: entering someone's life in an unwanted way. intrusive notifications.
  • selective: choosing carefully rather than taking everything. selective use of social media.
  • credible: worthy of belief. a credible source.
  • distorted: changed so that it gives a misleading impression. a distorted picture.
  • accountability: responsibility for decisions and their effects. platform accountability.
  • deliberate: done by conscious choice. deliberate screen use.

Form + purpose route: Argue in an article: establish the useful role of technology → examine a specific drawback → weigh a proportionate response → reach a judgement.

Phrase in context: Explain in a talk: "A notification interrupts more than a screen; it can interrupt a train of thought."

Environment and shared resources

  • sustainable: able to continue without exhausting the resources it depends on. a sustainable approach.
  • wasteful: using more than is needed. wasteful packaging.
  • biodiversity: the variety of living things in an area. protect local biodiversity.
  • conservation: protection of natural places, species or resources. a conservation project.
  • proportionate: appropriate in scale to the problem. a proportionate response.
  • long-term: considering an extended period. long-term consequences.

Form + purpose route: Persuasive speech: make the local problem concrete → explain why it matters → propose achievable action → invite specific participation.

Phrase in context: Formal letter: "A manageable reduction in waste would be more useful than an ambitious promise without a plan."

Health, wellbeing and balance

  • restorative: helping someone recover energy or strength. restorative time outdoors.
  • sedentary: involving a lot of sitting. a sedentary routine.
  • manageable: possible to deal with. manageable changes.
  • isolation: being separated from other people. reduce social isolation.
  • consistent: regular or not changing unpredictably. a consistent routine.
  • resilience: the ability to recover or adapt after difficulty. build resilience.

Form + purpose route: Advice article: acknowledge different circumstances → suggest small options with reasons → consider obstacles → encourage review, not perfection.

Phrase in context: Measured advice: "A routine should support your life, not become another source of pressure." Avoid unsupported medical claims.

Community and public space

  • inclusive: welcoming people with different backgrounds or needs. an inclusive community space.
  • neglected: not receiving enough care. a neglected park.
  • cohesion: people or parts holding together. social cohesion.
  • amenity: a useful local facility or feature. a valued public amenity.
  • consultation: asking for and considering people's views. meaningful consultation.
  • stewardship: careful responsibility for something shared. responsible stewardship.

Form + purpose route: Letter to a decision-maker: describe the place → explain its value to different users → examine the proposal → suggest an alternative or consultation → request action.

Phrase in context: Local article: "A space can belong to everyone on paper and still exclude people in practice." Explain the actual barrier.

Work, ambition and the future

  • adaptable: able to change to suit new conditions. an adaptable approach.
  • initiative: acting usefully without waiting to be told. show initiative.
  • vocational: connected with practical preparation for work. vocational training.
  • aspiration: a hope or ambition. support young people's aspirations.
  • transferable: useful in another setting. transferable skills.
  • precarious: uncertain or insecure. precarious employment.

Form + purpose route: Informative article: introduce the available routes → group practical differences → explain factors affecting the choice → summarise without treating one route as right for everyone.

Phrase in context: Advice letter: "Choose a route that fits your interests, while keeping your options open where possible."

Fairness, identity and belonging

  • prejudice: an unfair judgement formed without enough knowledge. challenge prejudice.
  • dignity: a person's worth and right to respectful treatment. treat people with dignity.
  • stereotype: an oversimplified fixed idea about a group. question a stereotype.
  • representation: how people or ideas are presented or included. fair representation.
  • marginalised: pushed to the edge of a group or society. marginalised voices.
  • mutual: shared between people. mutual respect.

Form + purpose route: Argumentative speech: identify an unfair assumption → explain its effects → challenge it with reasoning and a clear example → propose a fairer approach.

Phrase in context: Persuasive close: "Respect begins when we stop treating a label as an explanation." Do not make general claims about every member of a group.

Travel, leisure and local change

  • affordable: not too expensive for the people concerned. affordable transport.
  • reliable: able to be depended on. a reliable bus service.
  • congestion: overcrowding, especially of traffic. reduce congestion.
  • heritage: valued traditions or features inherited from the past. protect local heritage.
  • preservation: keeping something from damage or loss. the preservation of a historic site.
  • trade-off: a choice involving a gain and a loss. a practical trade-off.

Form + purpose route: Article weighing change: describe what is proposed → compare benefits and costs for different groups → consider alternatives → offer a justified conclusion.

Phrase in context: Formal request: "Before a permanent decision is made, residents should be able to understand the trade-offs."

5. Sentence patterns and paragraph movement

Choose the relationship first. Then choose a connector or sentence pattern that expresses it accurately.

  • Cause: "Because [cause], [effect]."
  • Consequence: "[Situation]. As a result, [consequence]."
  • Contrast: "[Point A]. However, [meaningful contrast]."
  • Qualification: "Although [limitation], [main point]."
  • Condition: "If [condition], [possible consequence]."
  • Specific example: "For example, [relevant detail]. This shows [connection]."
  • Change of scale: "For one student, [local effect]. Across a school, [wider implication]."
  • Add depth: "The problem is not only [surface issue]. It also affects [deeper issue]."
  • Develop rather than repeat: "This matters to [audience] because [new explanation]."
  • Return to purpose: "For that reason, [conclusion or action that follows]."
  • A purposeful short sentence: "That is the point."
  • A controlled semicolon: "The idea is promising; the practical details still need work."

A paragraph route you can vary

Idea → support → explanation → connection. Make a relevant point, support it with credible detail or a clear hypothetical example, explain its importance and connect it to the purpose or next idea. These moves can be combined, reordered or spread across paragraphs. They are not a compulsory acronym or four-sentence quota.

Storyline for non-fiction, not a forced story

Think of the piece as a movement in the reader's understanding: recognise the issue → understand what matters → consider the complication → see a useful way forward. You can use a brief anecdote to make the issue concrete, but do not turn an article, letter or speech into an unrelated fictional narrative. The audience and task remain in charge.

Make the register fit

Formal request: "I would welcome a discussion about a short trial." Teenage advice: "Try one small change before rewriting your whole routine." Speech to peers: "We can start with the space we already share." The subject can be similar while the relationship changes the voice.

6. Two full original worked examples

Read the choices, not a claimed grade. The examples are invented and unmarked, with annotations explaining their effects.

Original worked example 1: persuasive letter

Original practice brief: Write a letter to your headteacher persuading them to trial a short after-school library opening. This is an invented teaching task, not an OCR past question.

Dear Ms Patel,

When the final bell rings, the school library closes at almost the same moment that some students finally have time to use it. I am writing to ask whether we could trial opening it for half an hour after school on two afternoons each week. A quiet place to work should not disappear just when it becomes most useful.

For a student who shares a bedroom, or whose journey home involves waiting for a later bus, that short opening could make a practical difference. Imagine trying to finish a difficult paragraph with a younger sibling beside you and a television in the next room. The problem is not a lack of effort. Sometimes it is a lack of somewhere to put that effort.

The library could also help students ask for the right support. Someone who has forgotten a book could find an alternative; someone who has reached a dead end could at least leave with a clearer question for the next lesson. The aim would be focused, independent work, not another compulsory club.

I recognise that staffing and supervision cannot simply be wished into existence. That is why I am proposing a limited trial rather than an immediate permanent change. Students could sign up in advance, follow clear rules and help gather feedback. The school could then judge whether enough students use the space to justify continuing.

Would you consider discussing the proposal with the librarian and student council? If the trial proved impractical, we would understand why. If it worked, half an hour could turn an otherwise wasted wait into a useful part of the school day.

Yours sincerely,

A student

How this example is built

  • Opening: names a specific practical problem and a limited request. The headteacher can see what decision is being asked for.
  • Development: the hypothetical shared-room situation explains who might benefit and why. It does not claim to report a real survey.
  • Audience: the staffing paragraph responds to a decision-maker's likely concerns. A trial gives the proposal a practical shape.
  • Ending: asks for a discussion and explains how the proposal could be evaluated, rather than promising guaranteed results.
  • AO6: a short sentence sharpens the distinction between effort and space; a semicolon connects two related examples. These are purposeful choices, not required items.

Limit: this has not been examiner-marked. Its value is in the choices you can inspect, not a claimed level or score. No addresses are needed.

Original worked example 2: advisory article

Original practice brief: Write an article for a teenage magazine advising readers how to make more deliberate use of their phones. This is an invented teaching task, not an OCR past question.

Your phone does not need every spare minute

You sit down to answer one message. A few videos later, the short break has swallowed the time you meant to spend doing something else. If that sounds familiar, the answer need not be a dramatic promise to abandon your phone. It might be a smaller decision about when it deserves your attention.

Start by choosing one moment you want back. Perhaps it is the walk home with a friend, the first part of your homework or the last few minutes before you go to sleep. Trying to change every habit at once can make the task feel impossible. One clear boundary gives you something you can actually notice.

Then make that boundary easier to keep. If you want to work without checking messages, put the phone somewhere you cannot reach without getting up. If a group chat makes you feel that every message needs an immediate answer, tell your friends when you will check it. You are not rejecting them. You are making room to give one thing your full attention.

Of course, phones are useful. They hold travel plans, music, jokes and conversations we value. That is why a total ban may miss the point. The useful question is not whether screens are good or bad, but whether the way you are using yours matches what you meant to do.

Expect an imperfect start. If you catch yourself scrolling again, stop and reset rather than deciding that the whole plan has failed. At the end of the week, ask what changed. Did you finish work with fewer interruptions? Did a conversation last longer? Keep the boundary that helped and adjust the one that did not.

Your phone can stay part of your life without taking charge of every pause in it. Choose one moment. Keep it for yourself.

How this example is built

  • Opening: uses a recognisable situation and establishes a manageable approach rather than an absolute rule.
  • Sequence: choose a moment, change the surroundings, consider the complication, then review the result. The order is useful to someone trying the advice.
  • Audience: direct address and familiar situations suit a teenage magazine without assuming every reader has the same habits.
  • Balance: acknowledging useful phone functions makes the advice less blunt. It is relevant here, not a compulsory concession for every article.
  • Ending: returns to the idea of choosing a moment. The short closing sentences are complete sentences, not fragments.

Limit: practical teaching advice, not a medical claim or an examiner-graded answer. Another effective response could use a different order, voice or set of examples.

7. The final check

AO5: 24 marks

  • Did I answer this complete task, in its required form, for its stated reader?
  • Are ideas developed with explanation and relevant support rather than merely listed?
  • Does the voice suit the reader throughout? Is the organisation coherent?
  • Does the ending fulfil the purpose rather than merely announce that I have finished?

AO6: 16 marks

  • Are sentence boundaries clear? Have I corrected accidental comma splices and run-ons?
  • Are sentence structures varied where useful and controlled throughout?
  • Is vocabulary precise and suitable? Are punctuation and spelling accurate?
  • Have I checked agreement, apostrophes, homophones and omitted words?
Rules not to invent: no compulsory semicolon, fragment, tricolon, concession, headline formula, fixed paragraph count, single-argument limit or automatic whole-level form penalty. A letter needs a suitable greeting and sign-off, not addresses; never include your personal address. A short impressive extract cannot establish a whole-answer mark or qualification grade.

Practice now

Pick one original brief from the worked examples. Plan a different route, write in your own voice, then identify one AO5 improvement and one AO6 improvement. If you are simulating exam conditions, use only the materials your tutor has allowed and review this guide afterwards.
