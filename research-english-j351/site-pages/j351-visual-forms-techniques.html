How to use the models

Read the original practice brief above each page. Follow the numbered labels from the model into the annotation column. Keep the features that help your task; change the ideas, tone and order when your brief needs something different.

All people, publications, events and scenarios in the models are fictional teaching examples. Address fields are placeholders, not anyone's details. These are compact structure models, not prescribed answer lengths or examiner-marked scripts.

01 / Formal letter

Original practice brief: write to your headteacher to persuade them to trial a quiet after-school study room.

1[Sender's postal address]
[Town / city]
[Postcode]

[Date]
2Ms Patel
Headteacher
[School's postal address]
[Town / city and postcode]

3Dear Ms Patel,

Proposal: a quiet space after school

4I am writing to ask whether you would consider a short trial of an after-school study room. A quiet place to work could help pupils who need to wait for transport or cannot study easily at home.

5Your commitment to giving every pupil a fair chance is a strong starting point. A room available on two afternoons each week would turn that principle into a practical choice: somewhere to read, complete homework and ask for help without joining a formal club.

I recognise that supervision takes staff time. Rather than opening every day, the school could begin with a four-week trial, subject to staff availability. A simple sign-in record and a short feedback form would help assess demand before any longer commitment.

6Please could the student council discuss a manageable plan with you? The trial would not remove every barrier, but it could show whether a small change makes useful support easier to reach.

7Yours sincerely,

[Signature, if posting]
[Writer's name]

1Sender + dateTraditional postal position. Placeholders show the furniture without using personal information.

2RecipientName, role and delivery address identify who receives a posted letter.

3GreetingNamed recipient: Dear Ms Patel. Keep the tone respectful.

4Purpose firstState the request and the reader-relevant reason.

5Develop + adaptGrounded praise leads into a proposal. The next paragraph meets a practical objection.

6Specific next stepAsk for something the recipient can actually do.

7Matched sign-offNamed recipient: Yours sincerely. Dear Sir or Madam pairs with Yours faithfully.

In the OCR exam: OCR's June 2025 report says an appropriate greeting and sign-off are the layout essentials. Addresses do not affect the mark, but inventing them wastes time. Never use your personal address. The address blocks and signature here demonstrate postal convention, not exam requirements.

02 / Personal letter

Original practice brief: write to a friend who is struggling to find a suitable place to revise; offer practical advice.

1[Sender's address, if posting]
[Date]

2Dear Sam,

Thanks for your letter. You said that finding somewhere to revise has been harder than the revision itself. That sounds exhausting, especially when everyone keeps telling you simply to work harder.

3Before you change your whole routine, try changing one small part of it. Could you stay in the school library on the days when it is open? Even half an hour with a clear goal might be more useful than a long evening of interruptions.

Start with a task you can finish: one paragraph, a set of flashcards or the corrections from a lesson. A short list makes it easier to see what you have done, rather than ending the day worrying about everything still left.

4Of course, staying late may not fit your journey home. If it does not, ask a teacher whether there is a quiet room at lunchtime, or try agreeing a regular quiet period with the people you live with. Choose the option that is possible, not the one that sounds perfect.

5You do not need to sort out the whole term tonight. Pick one useful step for tomorrow, then see how it goes. Write back when you can; I would like to hear which option works for you.

6Take care,
Alex

1Light postal furnitureOne possible personal-letter layout. Date and address placement can vary.

2Individual relationshipFirst-name greeting and reference to a previous letter establish the context.

3Useful adviceOffer a specific action and explain why it may help.

4AlternativesRecognise limits. Advice should fit the recipient, not blame them.

5Reassuring closeReturn to this person and give a manageable next step.

6Personal endingA warm sign-off fits this relationship. Do not copy it into a formal complaint.

In the OCR exam: Let the task set the relationship. A letter to a friend can be warm and use contractions while remaining accurate and purposeful. This is a register variation within 'letter', not an extra required OCR form. No address is needed in the exam.

03 / Newspaper news article

Original practice brief: report a fictional study-room trial at invented Brookfield School. Scenario facts: four weeks, two afternoons, 45 minutes, supervised, capacity limited, reviewed afterwards.

The School Ledger
Campus news / Fictional practice edition

School to trial quiet study room

1Four-week scheme will offer pupils a place to work before their journey home.

2By Alex Reed / School correspondent

3Brookfield School will open a supervised study room on Tuesdays and Thursdays after lessons as part of a four-week trial next term.

4The room will be available for 45 minutes and is intended for pupils who need somewhere quiet to read or complete homework before travelling home.

Places will be limited to the room's safe capacity. Pupils will sign in on arrival and will be expected to work quietly. The scheme is not a taught revision class.

The proposal follows a student-council request for more places to study. The trial will test whether a small, regular opening is useful before the school considers a longer arrangement.

5Staffing and demand will be reviewed at the end of the four weeks. The room may not be available on days when supervision cannot be arranged.

6Pupils interested in taking part can ask their tutor for the trial arrangements. Transport home will remain each family's responsibility.

1Headline + standfirstThe large title gives the news; the smaller summary adds detail. The newspaper name is optional furniture.

2BylineShows who wrote it. A fictional name is used here; a byline is not an OCR requirement.

3News leadPut the main event and key facts first, rather than a long persuasive introduction.

4Supporting detailExplain who can use it and how it works. Columns imitate print, not an exam booklet.

5Limits + contextA report separates what is planned from what is certain.

6Practical endingUseful lower-priority information can finish a news report. A persuasive slogan would change the voice.

In the OCR exam: Match the purpose in the actual task. If asked to argue or persuade in an article, do not replace that with neutral reporting. Write normal paragraphs in the answer booklet; large type, columns, a masthead and a byline are not required. Every event/detail here is invented for the practice brief, not evidence to reuse as fact.

04 / Magazine or opinion article

Original practice brief: write an article for a school magazine arguing that pupils should have access to a quiet after-school room.

Student life / Opinion

A quiet room.
A fairer chance.

1Before we ask everyone to work harder, we should ask whether everyone has somewhere to work.

2Alex Reed / Original practice feature

3Picture the end of a school day: chairs scrape, bags close and the corridors empty. For some pupils, a quiet desk is waiting at home. For others, the search for somewhere to concentrate is only beginning.

That difference matters. Advice about revision often assumes that time and space are equally available. They are not. A shared room after school would not guarantee success, but it could make a useful starting point available to more people.

4Start small, judge honestly

No one is asking teachers to give up every afternoon. A short trial on two days a week would be a more realistic first step. Find out who uses it, ask what helps and check whether supervision is sustainable.

5Some pupils will prefer to work elsewhere; some will need transport arrangements the school cannot provide. Those limits deserve attention. They are reasons to design the trial carefully, rather than dismiss the idea before it begins.

6A quiet room cannot create equal circumstances. It can offer one fairer chance. Surely that is a door worth opening.

1Headline + angleA deliberate contrast establishes the viewpoint. The standfirst tells readers why it matters.

2Publication voiceA byline and section label make it look published. Both are optional.

3Reader connectionA hypothetical scene makes an abstract issue concrete; it is not claimed as real testimony.

4Subheading + turnOrganise a shift from the problem to a practical response. Subheadings are optional.

5ConcessionAccept genuine limits, then explain your judgement.

6Echo endingReturn to the opening idea with a developed conclusion, not an unrelated slogan.

In the OCR exam: A headline can signal the form, but the argument must do the work. An advice article would need actionable guidance; an informative article would need clear information. Do not force every article into opinion writing, columns or this exact paragraph order.

05 / Speech or talk

Original practice brief: speak to a school-council meeting to persuade members to support a study-room trial.

Quiet space, shared opportunity
Speech manuscript / School council meeting

1Good afternoon, councillors and teachers.

2Think about what happens when the final bell rings here. Some of us head straight home. Some wait for a bus. Some still need a quiet place to finish the work we have started.

What are we asking for? Not a new building, and not a promise to open every day. We are asking for a short, supervised study-room trial.

3First, consider the need. A desk, a chair and a little quiet can make a task feel possible. We cannot assume that everyone has those things waiting at home.

4Second, consider the limits. Staff time matters. So does the journey home. That is why I suggest two afternoons a week for four weeks, with numbers limited and arrangements reviewed.

A trial can show who comes. A trial can show what helps. A trial can show what needs to change.

5You may be wondering whether one room can solve a much wider problem. It cannot. But a small improvement does not become worthless because it is incomplete.

6Before we leave this meeting, please agree to ask the headteacher about a practical plan. Let us give this idea a fair hearing, and more pupils a fair chance.

Thank you.

1Locate the audienceGreet the people named in the task. Here, "this meeting" and "here" anchor the situation.

2Spoken openingInvite listeners into a shared situation. Hypophora poses and answers the central question.

3Audible signpostsListeners cannot reread a paragraph. Guide them through the line of thought.

4Rhythm + clarityControlled repetition helps a key idea land. Develop it with practical reasoning.

5Anticipate a concernAnswer an objection fairly instead of insulting those who disagree.

6Action + closeAsk this audience for a clear next step. "Thank you" supplies a natural ending.

In the OCR exam: Write the words intended to be heard. You do not need decorative script formatting, stage directions or a device in every paragraph. Speech and talk belong to the same listed form family; an informative talk may explain rather than campaign.

Connect and answer

Original adaptable examples. Choose the effect your reader needs. A device name is not a mark.

01. Flattery / positive recognition

Recognise an audience value before asking for support.

"Your care for this neighbourhood is clear. A safer crossing would put that care into practice."

Use with control: Useful in a request to a decision-maker. Keep praise credible; this is a hypothetical example, not evidence about a real person.

02. Shared values / identification

Connect a proposal to a concern the audience can share.

"We may disagree about the timetable, but we all want pupils to get home safely."

Use with control: Name a plausible shared aim. Do not pretend that everyone agrees with your solution.

03. Concession

Accept a reasonable point before giving your judgement.

"The scheme would take staff time. That is why a limited trial is more realistic than a permanent opening."

Use with control: Develop the answer to the concern. Adding "however" alone is not a rebuttal.

04. Anticipatory rebuttal

Raise a likely objection and answer it fairly.

"You may wonder who would supervise. The proposal should go ahead only if a safe rota is possible."

Use with control: Use a real difficulty, not a weak invented opponent.

05. Reframing

Change the question so the issue can be judged differently.

"Rather than ask whether teenagers care, ask whether they have a useful way to contribute."

Use with control: Explain why the new frame is fair. Do not dodge the original task.

06. Hypophora

Ask a question and answer it yourself.

"What would success look like? A service people can actually use."

Use with control: Useful for speech signposting. Avoid a whole paragraph of unanswered questions.

Pattern and emphasis

Original adaptable examples. Choose the effect your reader needs. A device name is not a mark.

07. Anaphora

Repeat a beginning across successive units.

"We need a safe route. We need a reliable service. We need a plan that lasts."

Use with control: The repetition creates emphasis; the different details must earn it.

08. Epistrophe

Repeat an ending across successive units.

"The timetable should work for pupils, the price should work for pupils, and the route should work for pupils."

Use with control: Use sparingly. Repetition without development becomes padding.

09. Parallelism

Use matching grammatical structures.

"Listen to the concern, examine the options, explain the decision."

Use with control: The matched verbs make the sequence easy to follow.

10. Tricolon / three-part structure

Group three connected units for rhythm or completeness.

"A useful space should be calm, accessible and safe."

Use with control: A three-part list is one possible pattern, not an automatic mark or a reason to add filler.

11. Antithesis

Place contrasting ideas in balanced form.

"We need fewer promises and more practical steps."

Use with control: Avoid suggesting there are only two choices when the reality is more complex.

12. Chiasmus

Reverse the order of key terms or ideas.

"We shape our routines; then our routines shape us."

Use with control: A memorable turn can open an explanation. Do not let the neat wording replace it.

Precision and pictures

13. Understatement

Deliberately use a restrained description for emphasis.

"A timetable that leaves pupils stranded is less than ideal."

Use with control: May sound wry. It can trivialise serious harm, so judge the subject carefully.

14. Litotes

Use a negative to express an affirmative, often with restraint.

"A quiet place to work is no small advantage."

Use with control: This suggests a substantial advantage. Repeated negatives can cloud meaning.

15. Self-correction / metanoia

Revise a phrase to sharpen or strengthen it.

"The delay is inconvenient. More accurately, it shuts some people out."

Use with control: Use when the correction adds precision, not merely drama.

16. Cumulative detail

Build emphasis by adding concrete, connected details.

"A missed bus means another wait, a late arrival and a homework session squeezed into the evening."

Use with control: Keep consequences plausible. This is a hypothetical chain, not a reported case.

17. Analogy

Explain an unfamiliar relationship through a clearer one.

"A budget is a short blanket: pulling more towards one area leaves less for another."

Use with control: Explain the relevant similarity and acknowledge where the comparison stops working.

18. Extended metaphor

Sustain a comparison across connected ideas.

"A plan needs foundations: clear costs, safe staffing and a timetable that will hold."

Use with control: Keep the comparison consistent. Avoid mixing foundations with sailing or machinery.

Control and structure

Original adaptable examples. Choose the effect your reader needs. A device name is not a mark.

19. Anecdote / hypothetical vignette

Give an issue a human-scale scene.

"Imagine reaching the library just as the lights go out and the doors close."

Use with control: Clearly signal an imagined example. Do not invent personal experience, quotations or research as fact.

20. Concrete imagery

Choose precise sensory detail rather than abstract complaint.

"The shelter has no roof; rain gathers on the only dry-looking seat."

Use with control: Useful in a hypothetical scene. Specific detail should support the purpose, not turn every task into a story.

21. Pronoun shift

Move deliberately between individual and collective focus.

"You can suggest a change. Together, we can decide which change is workable."

Use with control: Identify who "we" includes. Do not assume that the whole audience shares your experience.

22. Qualification / calibrated modality

Match certainty to the evidence and conditions.

"A trial could improve access, provided that supervision can be arranged."

Use with control: Could and may express possibility. Do not weaken every claim or promise a result you cannot establish.

23. Periodic sentence / delayed main clause

Build towards the main point after a controlled opening.

"If the room is safe, if staff are available, and if demand is clear, we should trial it."

Use with control: Make the final main clause complete. Length alone is not sophistication.

24. Call-back / circular ending

Return to an opening image with a changed understanding.

"Opening: a locked door. Ending: Let us find out whether we can open it."

Use with control: The conclusion should add a judgement or next step, rather than repeat the opening word for word.

Choose, adapt, check.

1. Name the job

Write a one-line brief: I am writing a [form] for [audience] to [complete purpose]. Include every linked request, such as explaining a problem and suggesting a response.

2. Choose the useful furniture

Letter: appropriate greeting and sign-off. Article: a relevant headline can help. Speech/talk: audience-aware spoken language. Do not spend exam time drawing columns, inventing addresses or designing a masthead.

3. Build a line of thought

Start with the reader's need. Develop each important idea with reasoning, a concrete example or a practical consequence. Change direction when the argument needs it, not after a fixed number of sentences.

4. Select a technique, then test it

Weak flattery: You are the best headteacher in the world, so you must agree.

More purposeful: Your commitment to pupil wellbeing gives us a useful starting point. Could we discuss a trial that supports it?

The second version links recognition to a relevant request. It still needs reasons and a workable plan. Neither sentence proves anything about an actual headteacher.

5. Read as the recipient

Is the tone appropriate? Is the action or explanation clear? Have I answered the whole task? Are paragraphs connected? Do the sentences and punctuation work? Have I claimed evidence I do not have?

Two-minute practice: choose a model. Keep its purpose but change its audience. Rewrite the greeting/headline, one developed paragraph and the ending. Identify which language had to change, and why.

Structure that earns its place

Separate final section / AO5 structure and AO6 language. The descriptors below are paraphrased from OCR's June 2025 J351/01 writing mark scheme, not a new marking grid.

Two independent judgements: AO5 is out of 24; AO6 is out of 16. AO5's highest band is Level 6 (21–24); AO6's highest band is Level 4 (13–16). Those band numbers are not GCSE grades. Examiners judge the whole response by best fit; a sentence or device cannot earn a fixed number of marks.

AO5: deliberate form, audience and purpose

Descriptor focus, paraphrased: the strongest responses shape the form around the reader and sustain a deliberately chosen tone, style and register.

Visible choice: the formal letter on page 2 addresses a headteacher with a feasible request, recognises staffing limits and ends by asking for discussion. The personal letter on page 3 offers alternatives without blaming the friend. Neither voice would fit every task.

AO5: control the whole journey

Descriptor focus, paraphrased: control the overall structure and use paragraphs and grammar to connect ideas and create effects.

One route in the formal letter: request and need → a concrete benefit → staffing concern and limited trial → specific next step. Each paragraph changes the job being done. The concern comes before the final request so that the request feels considered, rather than bolted on.

Contrast: the news article puts the event first, then details and limits. Copying the letter's persuasive route into that report would weaken its reporting purpose.

Coherence is the logic; cohesion is the linking

Loose: We need a room. Furthermore, teachers are busy. Moreover, there should be a trial.

Connected: Teachers are busy, so opening every day may be unrealistic. A limited trial would test demand without making that commitment. This approach would also allow staffing to be reviewed.

The cause-and-response connection makes sense; the repeated idea and the reference this approach hold it together. Connectives help only when their meaning fits.

Planning test: write each paragraph's job in the margin during practice. If two jobs are identical, develop or combine them. If an idea arrives without preparation, change its position or explain its link. This is a revision method, not a required paragraph formula.

Fluent vocabulary, not a thesaurus

AO6 rewards controlled choices. In the top band, OCR asks for precise, subtle vocabulary that expresses complex ideas clearly, alongside accurate spelling of irregular and ambitious words with very occasional lapses. This is a paraphrase, not a word list.

What does fluent mean here? Use language naturally, accurately and consistently for this audience. The checked mark scheme does not allocate a separate pot of marks called vocabulary fluency. It judges vocabulary, sentence choices and accuracy within AO6, while tone and register also matter to AO5.

1. Replace vague meaning, not every familiar word

Vague: The plan would make things better for people.

Precise: The trial could give pupils without quiet space at home a regular place to study.

Why it helps: identifies the action, the group and the benefit. Could keeps the outcome conditional. None of these needs to be a rare word.

2. Control meaning and collocation

Wrong fit: The proposal is feasible because it treats everyone fairly.

Repair: The proposal may be equitable because it directs help towards pupils with fewer study options. It is feasible only if safe supervision can be arranged.

Why it helps: equitable concerns fairness; feasible concerns whether something can be done. The explanation earns the distinction, rather than displaying two impressive-looking adjectives.

3. Match vocabulary to the recipient

To a headteacher: Please consider a supervised trial, subject to staff availability.

To a friend: Could you ask whether there is somewhere quiet to work after school?

Why it helps: the formal version is measured and operational; the personal version is direct and supportive. Both are clear. Neither is automatically better.

4. Preserve consistency and spelling

Do not jump from I would welcome the opportunity to this plan is totally epic without a reason that suits the voice. Practise useful spellings in phrases: accessible space; necessary supervision; a separate room; a realistic opportunity; accommodate different needs.

Check an unfamiliar word's meaning and normal partners in revision. In an exam, use a precise word you can control rather than gamble on one you cannot.

Sentence range with control

AO6 top-band focus, paraphrased: an ambitious range of sentence structures shapes meaning and impact. The next band also expects a wide range used deliberately. Range means useful variation in grammar, not merely alternating long and short sentences.

Short complete sentence

The room is available.

A short declarative can establish a fact or give emphasis.
Accuracy check: A full sentence needs a complete clause here. "A quiet room." is a fragment, not a safe substitute for sentence control.

Coordination

The room is small, but it could meet a real need.

Balances a limitation against a benefit.
Accuracy check: Both sides of but can stand as clauses. Do not join them with a comma alone.

Concessive subordination

Although staff time is limited, a short trial may be possible.

Makes the concession part of the same sentence as the response.
Accuracy check: The although clause cannot stand alone as a complete sentence.

Relative clause

The trial, which would last four weeks, could test demand.

Adds an explanatory detail without losing the main point.
Accuracy check: The extra information is enclosed by a pair of commas. The main clause still works if it is removed.

Conditional sentence

If supervision can be arranged, the room could open twice a week.

Makes the proposal depend on an explicit condition.
Accuracy check: The if clause is followed by the main clause; keep the condition and result logically connected.

Controlled participial opening

Recognising the staffing limits, the council proposed a short trial.

Connects the reasoning to the decision economically.
Accuracy check: The council must be the one recognising the limits. Avoid a dangling opening such as "Recognising the limits, the room opened."
Not a quota: these are six available choices, not six structures you must insert. A complex sentence that loses its grammar does not demonstrate control.

Punctuation, grammar and the final check

AO6 top-band focus, paraphrased: punctuation is accurate, improves clarity and creates particular effects. Check agreement and tense as well: "The proposal offers", "The pupils need". Accuracy and purpose matter together; a collection of punctuation marks is not a mark-scheme requirement.

Full stop: repair a comma splice

Wrong: Staff time is limited, a trial is possible.

Repair: Staff time is limited. A trial is possible.
Accuracy check: Each main clause can stand alone. A full stop separates them clearly.

Comma: show a boundary

After the trial, the school can review demand.

The opening phrase is separated from the main clause.
Accuracy check: Do not write "The school, can review demand." A comma should not split this subject from its verb.

Semicolon: link complete clauses

Staff time is limited; a short trial may still be possible.

The two independent clauses are closely related.
Accuracy check: Both sides must be complete clauses in this use. A semicolon is not a decorative comma.

Colon: introduce an explanation

The proposal has one condition: safe supervision.

The complete opening clause points forward to the condition.
Accuracy check: Do not force a colon after a verb, as in "The conditions are: safety and staffing."

Apostrophes: possession and omission

One pupil's bag; several pupils' bags; it's open; its door.

Singular possession: pupil's. Plural possession: pupils'. It's means it is; possessive its has no apostrophe.
Accuracy check: These are short grammar examples, not one model sentence. Ordinary plurals such as rooms do not take apostrophes.

Parentheses: extra information

The trial (subject to staffing) could begin next term.

The bracketed qualification adds a limit. The main sentence still works without it.
Accuracy check: Close the bracket. Use parentheses only when the aside helps; do not interrupt every sentence.
Final pass: check sentence boundaries, agreement, tense, apostrophes and the spelling of important words. Then read any long sentence once more for sense. These examples demonstrate choices, not marks awarded.
